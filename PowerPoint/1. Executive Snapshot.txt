Approach

I approached this task in four steps.

First, I reviewed the raw sales data and checked the main fields, such as dates, regions, customers, promotions, and sales reps.

Second, I cleaned and structured the data in SQL so I could create consistent reporting views.

Third, I used those reporting views in Power BI to build focused dashboard pages for executive KPIs, sales rep performance, promotion effectiveness, and data quality.

Finally, I used the outputs to build a short business narrative with practical recommendations, rather than only showing charts.




Build choises and why

A key design choice was to keep most of the transformation work in SQL and keep Power BI focused on visuals and lightweight measures.

I chose this approach because it makes the business logic easier to control, easier to explain, and easier to reuse.

I also used reporting views as a semantic layer. That means each dashboard page reads from a prepared view, instead of rebuilding the logic again inside Power BI.

This keeps the model simpler and reduces the risk of inconsistent KPI definitions.




Key assumptions

I made a few practical assumptions to complete the analysis.

First, I treated the available dataset as a valid short-term snapshot of sales activity from 24 to 30 April 2026.

Second, I assumed revenue is the main performance measure, because cost and margin data were not available.

Third, I assumed promotion effectiveness could be assessed through revenue, order volume, average order value, and revenue per unit.

Finally, I assumed that a one-week window is enough for a diagnostic view, but not enough for strong trend conclusions.




Data challenges and limitations

There were several data challenges during the work.

The first was date handling. The date key needed to be converted into a proper date format before it could be used correctly in Power BI visuals.

The second was model design. Because I used multiple reporting views, I had to be careful not to create incorrect relationships between aggregated tables.

The third was the date 29 April shows a very large revenue spike, but with only one week of data, I cannot say with confidence whether that is normal seasonality, a campaign effect, or a one-off event.

The main limitations are the short time window, the lack of cost or margin data, and the absence of broader historical context.

I included data quality checks in the analysis because decision-makers need to know not only what the numbers say, but also how much trust to place in them.



what i would do next
With more time and more data, I would extend this in four ways.

First, I would load a longer history so I could test whether patterns such as the 29 April spike are repeatable or exceptional.

Second, I would add cost and margin data so promotion analysis could focus on profit , not only revenue.

Third, I would rebuild the Power BI model on fact and dimension tables if cross-page filtering and deeper drill analysis were required.

Fourth, I would automate a weekly performance pack so leaders can track changes consistently over time.
